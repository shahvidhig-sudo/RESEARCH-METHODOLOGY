The 6 Systematic Stages in Formulating a Research Problem

FINER Criteria

How do researchers narrow down a vague, massive topic into a sharp, empirical, and testable research problem? Follow this six-stage hierarchical narrowing model with an applied nutrition example:

Stage I: Selection of a Broad Research Area Broad Field: Pediatric & Adolescent Nutrition in India

Begins with general professional curiosity, clinical observations, or high public health burden reported in national surveys.

Stage II: Dissecting the Broad Area into Sub-Areas Sub-Area Breakdown:

Dissect the field into operational sub-categories: (1) Macronutrient stunting/wasting, (2) Micronutrient deficiencies, (3) School meal schemes, (4) Adolescent iron deficiency, (5) Dietary diversity in rural hostels.

Stage III: Selecting the Specific Area of Interest Focused Sub-Field: Nutritional Iron Deficiency Anemia in Adolescent Schoolgirls

Researcher discards extraneous sub-areas to concentrate solely on iron bioavailability, dietary phytate inhibitors, and hemoglobin levels.

Stage IV: Generating Specific Research Questions Formulating Research Questions:

Q1: What is the baseline prevalence of microcytic anemia among adolescent schoolgirls in district X?
Q2: Does daily dietary consumption of an iron-fortified millet snack significantly improve endline hemoglobin over 12 weeks compared with standard snacks?

Stage V: Deciding Measurable Objectives Measurable Operational Objectives:

General: To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of bio-fortified millet supplementation.
Specific: (1) Estimate mean baseline Hb and serum ferritin; (2) Compare 12-week changes between fortified and control groups; (3) Assess dietary adherence via daily visual consumption logs.

Stage VI: Formulating the Final Statement of the Research Problem "A Double-Blind Randomised Controlled Trial Evaluating the Efficacy of an Iron-Fortified Pearl Millet Snack on Hemoglobin and Ferritin Status in Adolescent Schoolgirls Aged 12–16 Years in Gujarat."

Validates the FINER criteria: Feasible (local millet access), Interesting, Novel, Ethical (IEC approval obtained), and Relevant to national anemia elimination missions.

Data Presentation Styles: Graphical, Diagrammatic & Interpretation

Complete coverage of all syllabus visual display formats: One-dimensional (Bar diagrams), Two-dimensional (Pie charts), Histograms, Frequency Polygons, Cumulative Frequency Curves (Ogives), and rules for scientific interpretation of tables and figures.

One-Dimensional

Bar Diagrams

Width is arbitrary; length represents magnitude.

  • • Simple: Single discrete categories.
  • • Sub-divided / Component: Showing parts of a total (e.g. Carbs, Protein, Fat in calories).
  • • Multiple: Grouped comparisons (e.g. Boys vs Girls).
  • • Percentage Bar: Standardized to 100%.
Two-Dimensional

Pie & Area Charts

Both length and breadth are considered.

  • • Pie Diagram (Circle): Area partitioned into angular sectors proportional to shares.
  • • Sector Angle: $(\text{Value} / \text{Total}) \times 360^\circ$.
  • • Nutrition Use: National dietary calorie contribution by food groups.
Frequency Graph

Histogram & Polygon

Continuous numerical class intervals.

  • • Histogram: Adjacent touching rectangles; area equals frequency.
  • • Frequency Polygon: Midpoints of rectangle tops joined by straight lines.
  • • Frequency Curve: Smoothed freehand curve through polygon vertices.
Cumulative Curves

Ogives & Median

Cumulative frequency distributions.

  • • 'Less Than' Ogive: Rising curve starting from bottom-left.
  • • 'More Than' Ogive: Falling curve starting from top-left.
  • • Median Intersection: Point where both ogives cross yields the exact Median ($M$)!

Scientific Interpretation of Tables and Figures

In scientific research, raw tables and figures do not speak for themselves. Interpretation is the process of extracting physiological and empirical meaning, comparing results with theoretical expectations, and drawing bounded conclusions.

Table 1: 12-Week Dietary Intervention Outcomes Randomised Trial (N = 60)
Variable Control Diet (n=30) Millet Diet (n=30) p-Value
Baseline Hb (g/dL) 10.8 ± 0.6 10.9 ± 0.7 0.54 (NS)
Endline Hb (g/dL) 11.0 ± 0.7 12.6 ± 0.8 < 0.001*
Net Change (Δ Hb) +0.2 ± 0.4 +1.7 ± 0.6 < 0.001*
Serum Ferritin (μg/L) 14.2 ± 3.1 26.8 ± 4.5 < 0.0001*
1. Baseline Equivalence Verification:

Baseline Hb did not differ significantly between groups ($p = 0.54$), confirming that random allocation successfully balanced baseline nutritional status.

2. Quantitative & Statistical Inference:

The fortified millet group achieved an extra $+1.5\text{ g/dL}$ increase in Hb and nearly doubled serum ferritin ($26.8$ vs $14.2\,\mu\text{g/L}$, $p < 0.0001$). This confirms treatment efficacy.

3. Avoiding Unsupported Overreach (Scope of Generalization):

The conclusion is bounded to adolescent schoolgirls over 12 weeks. It cannot claim permanent lifetime eradication of all anemia or generalizability to pregnant adults without separate empirical trials.

Scientific Reporting, Steps in Writing & Types of Reports

Significance of report writing, the six formal steps in drafting, the distinct categories of scientific reports, and strict guidelines for thesis formatting.

TYPE 1 Academic Examination

Technical Report / Thesis

Exhaustive, comprehensive document written under faculty supervision for a master's or doctoral degree.

  • • Extended theoretical background and broad literature review.
  • • Complete operational descriptions of all apparatus, reagents, and SOPs.
  • • Comprehensive appendices: full raw codebooks, ethics certificates, consent forms, and questionnaires.
TYPE 2 Peer-Reviewed Journal

Journal Article (IMRaD)

Distills the thesis into a focused, highly reproducible scientific paper adhering to journal word counts.

  • • Introduction: Defined research gap and prespecified objective.
  • • Methods: Concise, reproducible experimental design.
  • • Results: Factual tables and figures without speculation.
  • • Discussion: Mechanistic explanation, limitations, and bounded implications.
TYPE 3 Public Health Policy

Popular & Policy Brief

Written for government health officials, NGOs, and the general public in clear, accessible prose.

  • • Focuses on actionable findings rather than mathematical formulas.
  • • Employs visual infographic summaries and executive policy recommendations.
  • • Translates biomarker improvements into community nutritional interventions.

Biostatistics, Probability & Data Collection Methods

Complete biostatistical foundations: classification of data, population vs sample, measures of central tendency, dispersion, probability distribution, schedules, questionnaires, and reliability.

1.1 Foundations

Types of Data & Sampling

  • • Qualitative Data: Descriptive non-numerical categories (e.g. food preferences, clinical signs of pallor).
  • • Quantitative Data: Numerical values (e.g. serum ferritin in $\mu\text{g/L}$, dietary sodium in $\text{mg/day}$).
  • • Population (Universe): Entire group of interest (e.g. all 10,000 pregnant women in a state).
  • • Sample: A representative subset selected for empirical investigation ($n = 400$).
1.2 Descriptive Stats

Central Tendency & Spread

  • • Mean ($\bar{x}$): Arithmetic average $\sum x / n$ (sensitive to extreme outliers).
  • • Median ($M$): Exact middle value of ordered observations (best for skewed income or vitamin levels).
  • • Mode: Most frequently occurring value (e.g. most popular hostel meal).
  • • Standard Deviation ($\sigma$): Root mean square deviation; measures dispersion around the mean.
1.3 Probability

Probability & Distributions

  • • Basic Probability: Ratio of favorable outcomes to total possible outcomes ($P = m/N$, bounds $[0, 1]$).
  • • Normal Distribution (Gaussian): Symmetrical bell-shaped curve where Mean = Median = Mode. $68.2\%$ lies within $\pm 1\sigma$; $95.4\%$ within $\pm 2\sigma$.
  • • Binomial & Poisson: Discrete probability models for event rates (e.g. incidence of foodborne illness outbreaks).

Syllabus Unit 2

Research Methodology, Study Design & Hypothesis Testing

Meaning and types of research, identification of problem, features of good design, concepts of variables, experimental vs control groups, hypothesis testing errors, and scientific reporting.

Features of Good Design & Concepts of Variables

Independent Variable (IV): The active factor manipulated by researcher (e.g. giving 500 mg Vitamin C vs Placebo).
Dependent Variable (DV): The outcome measured that changes in response to the IV (e.g. serum ferritin concentration).
Experimental vs Control Group: The experimental group receives the novel treatment; the control group receives identical baseline conditions (or placebo) to serve as a counterfactual reference.
Features of Good Design: Minimizes bias, maximizes experimental variance, controls extraneous variables (via randomization, blinding, matching), and exhibits high internal and external validity.

Survey Sample Sizing: Step-by-Step Cochran Formula

The Student Problem: Suppose you want to survey your college to determine "What percentage of hostel girls have anemia?". There are 3,000 girls in the college. You cannot draw blood from all 3,000 students! How many girls should you randomly test so your thesis supervisor and examiner will accept your findings as scientifically valid?

Click a Student Scenario to Automatically Load Parameters:
Confidence Level (Z-score): 95% (Z = 1.960)

Plain English Meaning: How sure do you want to be that this isn't accidental luck? In medical science, 95% sure (Z = 1.96) is universal.

Expected Prevalence (p): 30% (0.30)

Plain English Meaning: What % of people do you guess have this condition before you begin? If unknown, choose 50% for maximum variance safety!

Margin of Error / Precision (d): ±5% (0.05)

Plain English Meaning: Acceptable tolerance. With $\pm 5\%$, an observed 30% rate means the real population value is between 25% and 35%.

Non-Response Dropout (% Buffer): 10%

Plain English Meaning: Some students will be absent or decline. Inviting 10% extra guarantees you obtain your target completed sample!

"To estimate an expected 30% anemia prevalence with 95% confidence (Z = 1.96) and a 5% margin of precision, Cochran's formula yielded a minimum required sample of n = 323. Inflating for 10% anticipated non-response, a total of 359 students were invited."

Key Terminologies & Viva Quick Reference

Term / Acronym Formal Definition Applied Nutritional Context
ANOVA Analysis of Variance: Compares variation between groups vs within groups across ≥3 treatment arms. Comparing calcium absorption across Dairy, Soya, and Calcium Supplement groups.
Chi-Square (χ²) Non-parametric test comparing observed frequencies ($O$) with expected frequencies ($E$). Testing association between Vitamin A deficiency (Yes/No) and Bitot's spots (Yes/No).
Type I Error (α) Rejecting a true null hypothesis (false positive). Typically set at $\alpha = 0.05$. Claiming an herbal tea lowers cholesterol when in reality it had no true effect.
Type II Error (β) Failing to reject a false null hypothesis (false negative). Governed by statistical power ($1-\beta$). Missing a genuine blood pressure reduction from salt cutting due to a tiny sample size ($n=6$).
Standard Deviation (σ) Square root of variance: $\sqrt{\sum(x-\bar{x})^2 / (n-1)}$. Measures typical distance from mean. Quantifying variability in calorie intake across a hostel student cohort.
Action Research Coined by Kurt Lewin (1940); cyclical intervention to diagnose and solve immediate situational problems. Improving canteen vegetable hygiene practices after a localized food poisoning incident.
Positivism Founded by Auguste Comte (Father of Positivism); knowledge must be verifiable via empirical observation. Insisting on biochemical assays rather than subjective self-reported health feelings.
